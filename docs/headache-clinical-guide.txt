Headache — Clinical Guide
1. What is a headache?
A headache is pain or discomfort occurring in the head, scalp, face, or upper neck.
Clinically, headaches are broadly divided into:
A. Primary headaches
The headache itself is the disorder.
The major groups are:
1. Migraine
2. Tension-type headache
3. Trigeminal autonomic cephalalgias
    * Cluster headache
    * Paroxysmal hemicrania
    * SUNCT/SUNA
    * Hemicrania continua
4. Other primary headaches
B. Secondary headaches
The headache is caused by another disease, condition, substance, or medication.
Examples include:
* Head injury
* Meningitis/infection
* Intracranial bleeding
* Stroke
* Brain tumour
* Raised intracranial pressure
* Giant-cell arteritis
* Acute glaucoma
* Severe hypertension in appropriate contexts
* Sinus/ENT disease
* Cervical disorders
* Medication/substance effects
* Medication-overuse headache
The International Classification of Headache Disorders (ICHD-3) provides the formal classification. 

2. The three common primary headaches
Feature	Tension-type	Migraine	Cluster
Location	Usually bilateral	Often unilateral, can be bilateral	Strictly unilateral, usually around eye
Pain	Pressure/tightness	Pulsating/throbbing	Severe, sharp/burning/etc.
Severity	Mild–moderate	Moderate–severe	Very severe
Activity	Usually doesn't worsen it	Usually worsens it	Patient often restless
Nausea	Usually absent	Common	Usually absent
Light/sound sensitivity	Usually absent	Common	Usually not dominant
Eye/nose symptoms	No	Usually no	Common
Duration	≥30 min, can be continuous	4–72 h	15–180 min
Frequency	Variable	Variable	Up to 8/day during bouts
These patterns correspond closely to NICE and ICHD-3 diagnostic criteria.

3. Migraine
Migraine is a neurological disorder, not simply a "bad headache."
Typical migraine
A typical attack lasts 4–72 hours in adults.
Features include at least two of:
* One-sided pain
* Pulsating quality
* Moderate/severe intensity
* Worse with routine physical activity
And at least one of:
* Nausea/vomiting
* Sensitivity to light and sound
ICHD-3 uses these features to diagnose migraine without aura. 
Migraine with aura
Aura can cause temporary neurological symptoms before or during the headache.
Examples:
* Flashing lights
* Zig-zag lines
* Blind/blurred areas
* Tingling
* Numbness
* Speech disturbance
Typical aura symptoms are fully reversible and generally develop gradually. 
Migraine triggers
Common triggers can include:
* Sleep deprivation
* Excessive/irregular sleep
* Dehydration
* Skipping meals
* Stress
* Hormonal changes
* Bright light
* Certain foods
* Alcohol
* Caffeine changes
* Strong smells
Triggers are individual, so a headache diary is useful.

4. Tension-type headache
Typical characteristics:
* Bilateral
* Pressing/tightening
* Mild to moderate
* Not significantly worsened by normal activity
* Usually no vomiting
* Usually no prominent light/sound sensitivity
It can be:
* Infrequent episodic
* Frequent episodic
* Chronic
Chronic tension-type headache occurs on ≥15 days/month for >3 months. 

5. Cluster headache
Cluster headache is particularly important because its treatment is very different from ordinary headache.
Typical attack:
* Extremely severe
* One-sided
* Around/behind the eye or temple
* 15–180 minutes
* Can occur repeatedly, sometimes several times per day
* Patient is often restless rather than lying still
Associated symptoms on the same side:
* Red eye
* Tearing
* Runny/blocked nose
* Eyelid swelling
* Facial sweating
* Drooping eyelid
* Constricted pupil
ICHD-3 describes attacks occurring from once every other day up to 8/day. 

6. Secondary headache — the dangerous category
This is the most important thing your chatbot should learn.
Before saying:
"This is probably migraine."
it should first ask:
Could this be a secondary headache requiring urgent evaluation?

7. RED FLAGS 
A headache needs urgent medical assessment when concerning features are present.
Important red flags include:
Sudden thunderclap headache
Headache that:
* Starts suddenly
* Reaches maximum intensity within minutes
This can indicate serious conditions such as subarachnoid haemorrhage.
NICE specifically identifies sudden-onset headache reaching maximum intensity within 5 minutes as requiring assessment. 
Neurological symptoms
Examples:
* New weakness
* Facial drooping
* New numbness
* Speech difficulty
* New visual loss
* Confusion
* Reduced consciousness
* New seizure
* Significant balance problems
Infection features
Headache +:
* Fever
* Neck stiffness
* Altered consciousness
* Severe illness
can raise concern for meningitis/other infection.
Head trauma
New headache following significant head injury requires appropriate assessment.
Progressive headache
Especially:
* New headache
* Increasing frequency/severity
* New neurological symptoms
* New cognitive/personality changes
Eye emergency
Headache associated with:
* Severe eye pain
* Red eye
* Visual disturbance/haloes
* Nausea/vomiting
can indicate acute angle-closure glaucoma.
Other important situations
Consider secondary causes with:
* New headache in older age
* Pregnancy/postpartum state
* Cancer/immunosuppression
* Significant systemic illness
* Headache triggered by exertion/cough/straining
* Positional headache
* New headache with papilloedema or suspected raised intracranial pressure
NICE recommends further investigation/referral when concerning features such as fever with worsening headache, sudden onset, new neurological deficit or cognitive dysfunction occur. 

8. What should a doctor ask?
This is extremely important for your chatbot.
Use a structured HEADACHE HISTORY.
A — Age/onset
Ask:
* Age?
* When did headaches begin?
* First-ever headache?
* Sudden or gradual onset?
* Was it maximal immediately?
B — Location
Ask:
* Where exactly?
* One side or both?
* Behind eye?
* Forehead?
* Temple?
* Back of head?
* Neck?
C — Character
Ask:
* Pulsating?
* Pressure?
* Tightening?
* Sharp?
* Burning?
* Stabbing?
D — Duration
Ask:
* How long does one attack last?
* Seconds?
* Minutes?
* Hours?
* Days?
E — Frequency
Ask:
* How many headache days per month?
* How many attacks per day?
* Is the headache continuous?
F — Severity
Use:
0–10 pain scale
G — Associated symptoms
Ask about:
* Nausea
* Vomiting
* Photophobia
* Phonophobia
* Aura
* Fever
* Neck stiffness
* Eye redness
* Tearing
* Nasal congestion
* Weakness
* Numbness
* Speech problems
* Vision problems
* Confusion
H — Triggers
Ask:
* Sleep changes?
* Meals?
* Dehydration?
* Stress?
* Exercise?
* Cough?
* Sexual activity?
* Menstruation?
* Alcohol?
* Caffeine?
* Screen exposure?
I — Medication
Ask:
* What medicine?
* Dose?
* How often?
* How many days per month?
* Does it work?
This last part is particularly important because frequent use of acute headache medication can itself contribute to medication-overuse headache. 

9. Physical examination
A doctor should consider:
Vital signs
* Blood pressure
* Pulse
* Temperature
* Respiratory rate
* Oxygen saturation when clinically appropriate
Neurological examination
Assess:
* Mental status
* Cranial nerves
* Pupils
* Eye movements
* Visual fields/vision when indicated
* Facial symmetry
* Motor strength
* Sensation
* Reflexes
* Coordination
* Gait
* Speech
Other examination
Depending on presentation:
* Neck stiffness
* Temporal artery tenderness
* Eye examination
* Sinus/ENT examination
* Cervical spine
* Fundoscopy for papilloedema where appropriate

10. Does every headache need CT/MRI?
No.
A typical primary headache with a normal neurological examination generally does not automatically require brain imaging merely for reassurance.
NICE specifically advises against neuroimaging solely for reassurance in people already diagnosed with migraine, tension-type, cluster or medication-overuse headache.
Imaging/testing is driven by the clinical presentation and red flags.
Possible investigations depending on suspected cause:
* CT head
* MRI brain
* CT angiography
* MR angiography
* Lumbar puncture
* Blood tests
* ESR/CRP
* CBC
* Electrolytes/metabolic tests
* Pregnancy testing when appropriate
* Eye examination
The test should be selected based on the suspected diagnosis rather than simply because the patient has a headache.

11. Treatment — important distinction
There are two major treatment goals:
Acute treatment
Treat the current attack.
Preventive treatment
Reduce:
* Frequency
* Severity
* Disability
* Need for acute medication

12. Migraine acute treatment
Depending on the individual patient and contraindications, guideline-supported options include:
Simple analgesics
* Paracetamol/acetaminophen
* NSAIDs
Migraine-specific
* Triptans
For example, NICE recommends an oral triptan plus an NSAID or paracetamol for many adults with migraine.
Anti-emetics
Examples include:
* Metoclopramide
* Prochlorperazine
These can be useful when nausea/vomiting is present and in some circumstances even without prominent nausea.
Do not automatically recommend triptans to everyone. Cardiovascular disease, pregnancy, drug interactions and other factors can change the choice.

13. Migraine prevention
Preventive treatment may be considered when migraine is frequent, disabling, prolonged, or inadequately controlled with acute therapy.
Examples include:
* Propranolol
* Topiramate
* Amitriptyline
* CGRP-targeting therapies
* OnabotulinumtoxinA for selected chronic migraine patients
NICE currently recommends considering propranolol, topiramate or amitriptyline after discussing benefits, risks and suitability. 
Important: Topiramate has specific pregnancy-related safety restrictions and should not simply be recommended without assessing pregnancy potential and appropriate safeguards. 

14. Tension-type headache treatment
For acute treatment, guideline options include:
* Paracetamol
* NSAID
* Aspirin in appropriate adults
But:
Opioids should not be used for routine treatment of tension-type headache.
For chronic tension-type headache, preventive/non-drug strategies may be considered.

15. Cluster headache treatment
This is very different.
Acute attack
Guideline-supported treatments include:
* High-flow oxygen
* Subcutaneous or nasal triptan
NICE recommends 100% oxygen with an appropriate non-rebreathing mask and reservoir system, and triptan therapy where appropriate. 
Ordinary:
* Paracetamol
* NSAIDs
* Oral triptans
* Opioids
are not recommended as acute cluster-headache treatment. 
Prevention
Verapamil is commonly used during cluster bouts, with specialist oversight and ECG monitoring because of cardiac conduction risks. 
16. Medication-overuse headache
This is extremely important for your chatbot.
A patient may start with migraine and then develop increasingly frequent headaches because acute medications are being used too often.
NICE says to suspect medication-overuse headache when headache develops/worsens with ≥3 months of frequent use, including:
* Triptans/opioids/ergots/combination analgesics: ≥10 days/month
* Paracetamol/aspirin/NSAIDs: ≥15 days/month 
Treatment generally involves withdrawal of the overused acute medication, with clinical support and management of the underlying headache disorder. 

17. Non-medication management
A doctor should not only ask:
"Which tablet?"
Consider:
* Regular sleep
* Regular meals
* Adequate hydration
* Exercise as appropriate
* Stress management
* Trigger identification
* Caffeine management
* Headache diary
* Appropriate lifestyle changes
* Psychological/behavioral approaches when indicated
A headache diary should record things such as:
* Date
* Start time
* End time
* Severity
* Location
* Symptoms
* Possible triggers
* Menstrual relationship where relevant
* Medication
* Response to medication
NICE recommends considering a headache diary for frequency, duration, severity and treatment effectiveness.

19. What your medical chatbot should NOT do
This is especially important given your previous medical-chatbot project.
It shouldn't answer:
"You have migraine. Take sumatriptan."
from a single message.
Instead:
"Your symptoms may be compatible with migraine, but I need to check several things first."
Then ask the appropriate questions.
The chatbot should distinguish:
Emergency → urgent assessment
Possible secondary headache → medical evaluation/investigation
Likely primary headache → appropriate management
Uncertain → don't over-diagnose
